History

In brief. The Desert de les Palmes is a Discalced Carmelite monastic complex in Benicàssim (Castellón). The friars bought the land between 1691 and 1694 and founded a «desert» there: a secluded place for combining community life with long periods of solitary retreat. The convent survived all the nineteenth-century confiscations and is now part of a protected natural area.[1][2]

What «desert» means and why «de las Palmas»

For the Carmelites, a «desert» is not an arid place but an uninhabited spot where the Order's original spirit could be recovered: community life combined with an ideal of the hermit renewed by the Counter-Reformation.[1]

The name «Desierto de Las Palmas» is documented as early as 1697, alongside its Valencian form, «Desert de Les Palmes».[2] Some popular sources link it to the abundance of dwarf fan palms (palmitos, or margalló in Valencian) in the area,[3] a detail that should be checked.

It helps to distinguish two things. The name originally refers to the convent's property: the protected historic complex covers about 320 hectares.[1] The natural park, about ten times larger, took its name from the Desert in 1989.[2]

The search for the place (1625–1691)

When the Discalced Carmelite province of Aragon-Valencia split from that of Catalonia in 1625, it was left without a desert. After the search failed in Aragon, it was tried in Valencian lands, until a priest from Cabanes told the procurator of the Valencia convent, Friar Juan de la Virgen, that he knew a very suitable place.[1]

Purchase and foundation (1691–1694)

In 1691 the Gavarrell farmhouse was bought; on 2 February 1694 the deed of possession was drawn up and the rest of the land was acquired for 23,000 libras.[1] It was, therefore, a purchase.

The foundation had to overcome three kinds of opposition: that of the Aragonese Carmelites, who wanted the desert in their own territory; that of the Baron of Benicàssim, who defended his feudal rights over the district; and that of the Franciscans of Castellón. The dispute was settled on 23 December 1693 with a royal licence in favour of the convent.[1]

The old convent and the hermitages (1694–1783)

The farmhouse proved unhealthy and it was decided to build a convent in the Bancal de la Colada. Works began in 1698, with Friar Bernardo de San José as designer (the one who draws up the plans) and Pedro Vilallare as master builder. The building was finished around 1709, although the church was not completed until 1733.[1]

Around the convent, hermitages (up to thirteen) and small structures called «antros» appeared, inspired by the caves of the first hermits, where a brother could withdraw for long periods. Between 1724 and 1730 the wall enclosing the site was built.[1] According to the protection file, the prior visited the hermitages once a week and the hermits came down to the convent on Sundays and feast days.[1]

Around 1710, during the War of the Spanish Succession, bands of «micaletes» harassed the friars and even shut them in over a matter of concealed weapons.[1]

The first inhabitants

According to a 1915 book by the Carmelite Pedro de la Madre de Dios (in the Spanish version by Juan Bautista Felíu Saera), the first prior was the Aragonese Friar Miguel de San José. Two lay brothers lived with him: Bartolomé de la Santísima Virgen, who looked for a cave on the mountain, and Juan de Todos los Santos, who found another at the Peña Corva. A note by the translator explains that the popular name «el Bartolo», for the Sant Miquel hermitage and the mountain, comes from Friar Bartolomé.[4]

The 1783 rains and the New Monastery

Between September and December 1783, torrential rain opened cracks in the convent walls and sinkholes in the ground. The friars moved into a guesthouse and, after seeking advice, the burial vault and the western buildings were demolished so that their materials could be reused in a new convent on the Portería terrace.[1]

Works began in 1784 under the direction of Friar Joaquín del Niño Jesús, a master builder trained in Zaragoza, whose project was approved by the Royal Academy of San Carlos in Valencia on 16 January 1785. The church was begun in 1791, the eremitic life resumed in 1793, the church was finished in 1796 and in 1802 the remains from the old convent were moved to the new monastery.[1]

The 19th century: a convent that held out

The 19th century was turbulent for religious orders, and the Desert came through without being suppressed. In 1811, with the arrival of General Suchet, its goods were inventoried for seizure; the prior hid part of them. In 1813 there was an attempt to sell the Desert. The sources tell this episode somewhat differently: the protection file says it was thwarted by an unfavourable report from the mayor of Benicàssim,[1] while Father Husillos's study suggests the friars recovered the property through a third party friendly to the community.[2]

The following attempts at confiscation (the Liberal Triennium of 1820–1823, Mendizábal's in 1835–1836 and Madoz's in 1855) also failed to end the convent. During the 1834 cholera epidemic the friars offered to care for the sick of Castellón, and in 1835 Castellón town council asked the captain general not to suppress the convent, a request that was granted. Of the four Carmelite convents in the Kingdom of Valencia, the other three disappeared after 1836.[2]

The community shrank greatly: in 1873 only the prior and three or four fathers remained.[1] With the Restoration the situation changed: having never been exclaustrated placed the monastery in a prominent position in Spain, and in 1880 it was declared a novitiate, with a consequent growth of the community.[1]

From the liqueur to the civil war

To overcome financial hardship, the monks began gathering aromatic herbs from the Desert to distil a liqueur. The Carmelitano liqueur business was inaugurated on 15 October 1896, when Father Arcángel de la Virgen del Carmen was prior.[2] Production was first set up in the convent cellars, then in a nearby building and, in 1913, in the factory in Benicàssim.[1]

In the civil war of 1936, sixteen monks of the community were killed.[1]

The 20th century and today

In 1971 a spirituality centre was created. The building of the road, first from Castellón and later also from Benicàssim, brought large numbers of visitors. Forest fires have been another constant scourge of the area.[1]

Legal protection came in stages: Decree 149/1989 of 16 October declared the Desert de les Palmes a Natural Area; Decree 95/1995 approved its Master Plan for Use and Management; and in 2004 proceedings began to declare the Desert an Asset of Cultural Interest in the category of historic site.[1]

What survives

The New Monastery forms a quadrilateral of 53 × 41 metres, with three storeys and a basement, built of rodeno (red sandstone) rubble masonry and lime mortar. Only ruins remain of the old convent, because its materials were reused. The protection file also lists 18 hermit caves (antros), 10 hermitages, 9 devotional pillars, stations of the cross, pens, springs (those of San José, San Juan de la Cruz, la Teja and el Panteón, among others) and a stretch of the Portería Alta wall, about 3.75 metres high.[1]
